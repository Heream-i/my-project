The Great Legal Asymmetry

How the State Weaponised "Ignorance of the Law" Against Citizens While Insulating Itself from Failure

The British legal tradition was founded on a simple, sacred promise: laws exist to protect ordinary people from arbitrary power, tyranny, and harm. Yet, inside the modern planning and building industry, that relationship has been completely inverted. We now live under an engineered divide—a strict system of Us vs. Them—where the law is used as a punitive trap for the citizen, and an absolute shield for the state.

Us: The Ordinary Citizen

Strict Liability: Ignorance of the law is no excuse. If you or a rogue builder bypass a complex regulation—even completely unawares—the state demands total accountability. You face financial ruin, forced demolition, and criminal prosecution.

Them: The Public Authorities

Absolute Immunity: Systemic ignorance is legally excused. If a council or building inspector signs off on dangerous, non-compliant work due to oversight or incompetence, the courts rule they owe you no "duty of care." They walk away completely insulated.

The History of Subversion: How We Lost Our Rights

This asymmetry is a betrayal of centuries of British constitutional history. The laws governing this land were originally written to establish our rights and curb the immunity of those in power.

1215 – Magna Carta

The foundational bedrock of British liberty. It explicitly established that the Crown is not above the law. It promised that justice could not be sold, delayed, or denied, and protected ordinary subjects from arbitrary state overreach.

1689 – The Bill of Rights

Cemented the rule of law over the absolute power of the Monarch. It established that public authorities cannot suspend laws or execute policies without explicit parliamentary authority, specifically ensuring protection against arbitrary state penalties.

1765 – Entick v Carrington

A landmark common law ruling declaring that public officials have no inherent right to infringe upon a citizen’s home, life, or property unless explicitly justified by a clear, un-compromised law. The home was deemed an untouchable castle.

1936 – The Public Health Act

Parliament codified the modern state's explicit duty to protect its citizens. This legislation gave authorities clear powers to inspect, regulate, and guarantee the sanitation and structural integrity of housing. It was intended to act as a shield of public safety, ensuring that ordinary people could rely on the state's oversight to protect their lives and homes from hazardous practices.

1990 – The Great Judicial U-Turn (Murphy v Brentwood)

The historical turning point where the judiciary broke the contract. The House of Lords ruled that local authorities owe zero duty of care to individual homeowners when their building control inspectors negligently sign off on defective work. The state successfully stripped citizens of their right to sue for regulatory failures, transforming public safety duties into a fiction that protects only the state's wallet.

The Systemic Denial of Fundamental Human Rights

By forcing strict compliance on ordinary citizens while granting absolute civil immunity to its own regulatory bodies, the UK legal framework actively violates the Human Rights Act 1998 on a daily basis:

Article 1, Protocol 1: Protection of Property
Every person is entitled to the peaceful enjoyment of their possessions. When the state operates a regulatory system that certifies unsafe properties, and then forces the innocent homeowner to pay for those overlooked defects under threat of prosecution, it places an "individual and excessive burden" on the citizen, destroying the value of their property.

Article 6: The Right to Access Justice
By treating public regulatory failures as "non-justiciable" and barring citizens from seeking civil damages for inspection negligence, the state has effectively closed the courthouse doors to ordinary people, wiping away their right to a meaningful legal remedy.

Article 8: Right to Respect for the Home
A citizen’s home is the core of their dignity and security. The state fails its positive obligations to protect the home when it legally absolves itself of any duty to accurately enforce safety laws, shifting the toxic fallout of regulatory failures entirely onto families.

"The modern application of building regulations in the United Kingdom represents a perversion of natural justice. Ordinary citizens are held to a standard of strict, punitive compliance where honest ignorance is weaponised against them. Yet, the state grants its own authorities total immunity for their operational ignorance and structural failures. The safety net of public health has been converted into an authoritarian regulatory trap—demanding absolute compliance from the vulnerable while legally absolving itself of any duty to care to the very individuals who rely on its oversight every day."
